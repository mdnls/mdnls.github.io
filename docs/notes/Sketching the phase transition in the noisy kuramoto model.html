Sketching the phase transition in the noisy kuramoto model

Here is a concise sketch of the phase transition calculation for the noisy Kuramoto model. We will perform the calculation and then discuss some of the frills and red herrings which physicists tend to invoke when reviewing it.

We are interested in understanding phase transitions in the Gibbs distribution, $$ \begin{aligned} \rho_N(\Theta_N) \propto \exp \left( - \beta H_N(\Theta_N)\right) \qquad H_N(\Theta_N) \coloneqq \frac{-1}{2N}\sum_{i,j} \cos(\theta_i - \theta_j). \end{aligned} $$ for a range of $\beta > 0$ as $N \to \infty$. The point of this calculation is to witness a phase transition of the following type. Set $m_N(\beta) = \E[\frac{-1}{N} H_N(Z_N)]$. What we are expecting is that $m_N(\beta) \to m_\infty(\beta)$ and that $m_\infty$ looks like, At a critical temperature $\beta_c^{-1} > 0$, the mapping $\beta \mapsto m_\infty$ has discontinuous first derivative, its value is zero when $\beta \leq \beta_c$, and positive but finite when $\beta > \beta_c$. We discuss the interpretation of this below.

We will suppress the subscript $N$ on quantities whose norms are $O(N)$, but keep it for things that have a limit as $N \to \infty$. It is convenient to work with a reparametrization $Z = (e^{\iota \theta_1}, \ldots, e^{\iota \theta_N})$. Here $\iota$ is the complex unit, not to be confused with the index $i$. Then: $$ \begin{aligned} H(\Theta) & = \frac{-1}{2N}\sum_{i,j} \mathrm{Re}(e^{\iota(\theta_i - \theta_j)}) = \frac{-1}{2N} \left( \sum_{i} Z_i\right)\left( \sum_{i} Z_i\right)^* = \frac{-1}{2N} \left|\sum_i Z_i \right|^2. \end{aligned} $$ So we abuse notation and write $H(Z) = \frac{-1}{2N} |\sum_{i}Z_i|^2$.

Put an auxiliary parameter $s > 0$ and set $$ \begin{aligned} Z(\beta,s) & = \int \exp \left(- \beta s H(Z) \right) \, d Z \qquad F_N(\beta, s) = \frac{-\beta^{-1}}{N}\log Z(\beta, s). \end{aligned} $$ The plan is to calculate a limit object $F_N(\beta, s) \to F_\infty(\beta, s)$, and to then extract (a formal expression for) $m_\infty(\beta)$ using the relation $m_\infty(\beta) = -\partial_{s=1} F_\infty(\beta, s)$.

One can calculate (using a Gaussian integration trick called the "Stratonovich transform," refer to AI if curious) that, $$ \begin{aligned} Z(\beta, s) & = \frac{N}{\beta s} \int_0^\infty r e^{-N \Phi(r, \beta, s)} \, dr \qquad \Phi(r, \beta, s) \coloneqq \frac{r^2}{2 \beta s} - \log(2 \pi I_0(r)) \end{aligned} $$ Next, apply Laplace method (one must check that it applies) to see $$ \begin{aligned} F_N(\beta, s) &= \frac{-\beta^{-1}}{N} \log \int_0^\infty r e^{-N\Phi(r, \beta, s)}\, dr + \frac{-\beta^{-1}}{N} \log \left(\frac{N}{\beta s} \right) \\ & \to \beta^{-1} \inf_r \Phi(r, \beta, s) \end{aligned} $$ The stationary condition for $r^*(\beta, s) = \text{arg-inf}_r \Phi(r, \beta, s)$ is $$ \partial_r \Phi(r, \beta, s) = 0 \iff \frac{r}{\beta s} - \frac{I_1(r)}{I_0(r)} = 0.$$ In other words, $r^* = \beta s \frac{I_1(r^*)}{I_0(r^*)}$ and physicists say that $r^*$ solves the 'fixed point equation'. The point $r=0$ is always a stationary point, but not always the global minimum. In fact, $\beta_c$ is precisely the threshold at which $\beta > \beta_c \implies r^*(\beta, 1) > 0$. We discuss some interpretations of this below.

To summarize all of this:

  1. $F_N(\beta, s)$, known as the free energy, is an auxiliary object that is used to calculate $m_N(\beta, s)$, via a cumulant-generating-function-like approach.
  1. The general strategy is to rewrite $Z(\beta, s)$ as an one dimensional integral of type $\int e^{-N \Phi(r, \beta, s) + o(N)} \, dr$. The function $\Phi(r, \beta, s)$ carries significant physical meaning, discussed below.
  1. By Laplace method, the limit $F_\infty(\beta, s)$ is seen to depend crucially on $r^*(\beta, s) = \text{arg-inf}_r \Phi(r, \beta, s)$. Within the scope of physical interpretations of $\Phi(r, \beta, s)$, the locations of its local minima are of particular interest.
  1. Once we know $r^*(\beta, s)$, we just plug in its value to compute $F_\infty(\beta, s)$ and the derivative at $s=1$. We can now compute $m_\infty(\beta)$ pointwise for each $\beta$.

Now we discuss the physics interpretations.

First, observe that the quantity $F_N(\beta, 1)$ can also be rewritten, $$ \begin{aligned} F_N(\beta, 1) & = \frac{1}{N}\E_{\rho_N}[H(Z)] - \beta^{-1} \, \frac{-1}{N} \int \rho_N(Z) \log \rho_N(Z) \, dZ \end{aligned} $$ Physicists like this expression because it is similar to a quantity in statistical physics called the "Helmholtz free energy." It is a thermodynamic quantity $F$ solving the relation $F = U - TS$ for $S$ the entropy of the system, $T$ the temperature, and $U$ the internal energy of the system. This is why $F_N(\beta, 1)$ is called "free energy."

Second, let's discuss the meaning of $\Phi(r, \beta, s)$ and its minimizers. Physicists like to think of $r$ as a "macroscopic observable," which provides a coarse description of a realization $Z$, and they think of the density $\exp(-N\Phi(r, \beta, s) + o(N))\, dr$ as an (unnormalized) "macroscopic Gibbs measure." In the limit $N \to \infty$, $r$ concentrates on $r^*$, so $r^*$ is describing the typical "coarse" behavior of the system. (This is why Grigorios was treating $r^*$ as a meaningful object in lecture today).

Apparently, in the Kuramoto case, the direct relationship between $r$ and $\frac{1}{N}H(Z)$ is that $r = |x|$ where (conditionally on $Z$) $x$ is a complex Gaussian with mean $\frac{\beta s}{N} \sum_{i} Z_i$ and covariance $\frac{\beta s}{N}I_2$. Consequently, as $N \to \infty$, we have (again conditional on $Z$) that $\frac{r^2}{2(\beta s)^2} + \frac{1}{N}H(Z) \stackrel{d}{\to} 0$.

To explain a final piece of intuition that physicists use: very roughly, one can interpret $\beta^{-1} \Phi(r, \beta, s)$ as a "coarsened free energy." In the present case, call $h = \frac{r^2}{2 (\beta s)^2} \approx \frac{-1}{N}H(Z)$, and take $s=1$, so that $$ \begin{aligned} \beta^{-1} \Phi(r, \beta, 1) &= h - \beta^{-1} \log(2 \pi I_0( \beta \sqrt{2 h})) \end{aligned} $$ Physicists sometimes speak of an "energy-entropy tradeoff" in the context of phase transitions. What they mean by this is that the macroscopic Gibbs measure is of the present type, and further, that the coarsened free energy decomposes into an energy term (here $h$) and entropy term (here $-\log (2 \pi I_0(\beta \sqrt{2h}))$).
